# Case pitch guidance principles

## How to use these principles:

This guidance is intended to steer case pitches to success, but not limit ideas creatively. If you think you have a hilarious and inspired case pitch that doesn’t fit the guidance, pitch it. The ultimate goal is just to create value for the player. Maybe a few unorthodox outliers will elevate the set of cases as a whole.

## Case guidance principles

# Keep it simple

The player is expected to complete hundreds of cases over the course of the game. If each case is difficult to follow, that will just become mentally exhausting. The best case gets the player to the payoff as effortlessly as possible, so they can get that hit of entertainment value and then move right on to the next case.   
There may also potentially be larger story arcs layered over the many smaller cases, leading to the dramatic final conclusion of the game. If the cases are too complicated already, layering over the high-level arcs will just make them an impenetrable mess.  
After a pitch is selected, a second phase will detail out the mechanics of the case in terms of gameplay. If there’s a lot of superfluous detail in the pitch, it adds unnecessary burden during the mechanics phase. If the pitch is distilled down to just the essential value of the concept, it frees up this future phase to have more room to adapt the case in terms of what works mechanically. 

# Shoot for sardonic

	Many stories of the supernatural are played straight, with the audience connecting to the sincere drama of the characters. That is not the tone of this game. The game is intended to be a dry, borderline sarcastic comedy. The comedy isn’t intended to be cartoony slapstick (though some of that would be acceptable if it’s really funny.) It’s also not attempting to be moralizing or preachy. The joke of the game’s world is that people are willing to take these ridiculous, supernatural situations as they come. Even if ghosts and aliens and eldritch horrors aren’t “normal,” the fact that the investigators have this boring, low paying job is to investigate such things, leaves the investigators feeling like “okay. I guess this must be pretty normal.” They may approach an apocalyptic threat to humanity the way a plumber treats a particularly nasty plumbing problem. That’s the joke.  
	Over time, the player may grow fond of their investigators and then be emotionally invested in them by the time of the final conclusion of the game. But the cases should still be amusing even if the player chooses not to emotionally invest in the characters. 

# Maintain compatibility with other cases

	Any given case needs to be at least nominally compatible with all the other cases. It’s fine if something like a big monument is introduced in one case, and a scary monster is introduced in another case. A town can accommodate many such things. But if, in one case, it’s established that the town is an island on the back of a giant turtle, this has too many knock-off effects. Now the town has to have been on an island all along. If another case involves a gas-station just outside of town, it won’t make any sense.  
	It’s fine for disasters to befall the town, like an earthquake or a loss of gravity or the whole town becoming 2-dimensional. In these situations, the town just has to have a way back to the status quo. If the town is 2-dimensional for a while but then goes back to 3d, the case is contained. If the town stays 2-dimensional, or had to be 2-dimensional all along, that won’t work.  
If a bunch of people die in a disaster, but the town can theoretically just recover from that eventually, the case is contained. Part of the comedy is how comfortable the town is with just rolling with weirdness.
